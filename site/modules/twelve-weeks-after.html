Twelve weeks, with evidence in each block

The course is not the finish line. This plan spreads the practice over twelve weeks, and each block ends with an output you can point to. The plan is the course's advice. The weeks and outputs describe what to build, not results the course has measured.

WeeksFocusOutput
1 to 2Real evals for ReinTen tasks, baseline numbers, a README table
3 to 4One experiment and one extensionWrite-up number one, published
5 to 7Open-source contributionOne or two merged pull requests
8 to 9Eval studyWrite-up number two, with data
10 to 11Domain agent and securityA repository, a threat model, injection tests
12Mock interviews and applicationsFive mock sessions, and tailored applications

Set weekly goals that leave evidence. Hours are not a goal you can check. "Practice more" is not one either. "One merged pull request, linked from the write-up" is, because the reviewer can open the link.

Read the table as a sequence of checks, not a list of tasks. The first two weeks give you baseline numbers for Rein's own tasks. Weeks three and four turn those numbers into an experiment. Weeks five to seven put your work into a project you do not own. Each block depends on the one before, so a block that slips moves the ones after it.

Keep the plan on a schedule. The same hour each week and the same block order make it easy to see where you are, and easy to notice when a week slips.

Run a mock interview in the twelfth week, and as needed before it. A friend asks the questions and grades your answers against a rubric. Use the say-it prompts and the katas from earlier modules as the questions. The friend is the grader, so agree on the rubric before you start.

The first mock can be short. Its job is to show which question you cannot answer yet. Ask the friend to grade one answer aloud, so you hear the rubric in use before the real round.

After each mock, write down three things: the questions asked, your answer to each, and what to improve next time. A mock with no notes repeats its mistakes. The next one starts from your notes.

The course's plan for the daily on-call shift is about ten minutes: one incident, the due cards, and one micro-kata or say-it drill. A short shift each day keeps your cards and your skills in use. A missed day is not a failed plan.

Check your plan

Answer these from the plan above. Each correct answer names the output a reviewer could check. If an answer surprises you, change the plan before the week starts, not during it.

Without JavaScript, the question is: a week's goal is write more about my project. Which goal is evidence?

  1. One merged pull request, with its link in the write-up.
  2. More reading about the project.
  3. Feeling ready by the end of the week.

Without JavaScript, the question is: a mock interview ends. What should you write down?

  1. The questions, your answers, and what to improve next time.
  2. Only whether it felt good.
  3. Nothing, so the practice stays private.

Without JavaScript, the question is: you miss several days of the daily shift. What is the course's advice?

  1. Start again the next day, and do not try to clear the backlog in one sitting.
  2. Double the shift until the backlog is gone.
  3. Stop the plan, since a missed day ends it.

Say it out loud

  1. Run a two-minute mock. Walk me through Rein results and name the one change you would make next.
  2. Set out your plan for the next twelve weeks. What evidence does each block end with?

Keep it

Practice in public, on a schedule.

What makes a weekly goal evidence?
It ends in a link you can check: a merged pull request, a results table or a write-up.
What is a mock interview?
A practice interview in which someone else asks the questions and grades your answers against a rubric.
What should you write down after a mock interview?
The questions, your answer to each, and what to improve next time.

The course's plan: about ten minutes, with one incident, the due cards, and one micro-kata or say-it drill.
